# Using Your Studio

Everything in the studio serves six verbs: **sign in, ask, watch, review, accept, launch.** This
chapter walks them in order. If a page ever confuses you, come back here — and if this chapter
doesn't resolve it, that's a fault in the studio, not in you. Tell us.

## Who you're working with

First, meet your team. **Your team is AI — Claude — running on your venture's own machine**, around
the clock. It wakes every few minutes, looks at the work you've asked for, and gets on with it:
writing code, drafting content, researching. It is not a chat window that forgets you — everything
it does is written down in your venture's own records, and you can read all of it.

Two things your team will never do: it never decides *what* your product should be — that's you —
and it never sends anything to the outside world (an email, a post, money) without your recorded
say-so. Those two rules never bend.

## 1. Sign in

Your studio address is your venture's page. Sign in with your venture Google account, or with the
email and password you've been given. You'll land on your venture — and only yours.

## 2. Ask — telling the studio what you want

The button **"Tell the studio what you want"** opens the composer. This is how all work starts.

Write in plain English, the way you'd brief a capable colleague. Say what's bothering you or what
you want to exist — you don't need to know how it would be built:

> *"People lose their saved card lists when they sign back in — can we fix that?"*
>
> *"I want the brand to feel like a premium financial terminal, not a developer tool."*

The composer will look through what your venture already knows, sometimes check the wider market,
and then do something important: **it reads the work back to you before filing anything.** You'll
see what it understood, what it would do, and what it would leave alone — and often one sharp
question. Answer it honestly; that answer shapes the work. Nothing is filed until you say yes.

When you say yes, your ask becomes a **ticket** — a written piece of work with a name (like
ARCA-58), a scope, and a list of what "done" means. Tickets are how you and your team stay honest
with each other: the ticket is what you asked for, so later you can judge what came back against
it.

**Good asks** name one want at a time and say why it matters. **Vague asks** ("make it better")
get you a clarifying question rather than work — that's the composer doing its job, not being
difficult. You can also hand the composer a document — a price list, a pitch deck, some notes —
and it becomes part of what your venture knows.

## 3. Watch — what your team is doing

The board's **"What your team has been doing"** panel shows the work as it happens: what's being
worked on right now, when your team last checked in, and what finished recently.

Sometimes you'll see work marked **stuck — needs a human**. That's honesty, not failure: your team
tried something a few times, couldn't get it past its own quality checks, and stopped rather than
forcing something bad through. It's parked until a person looks. If you edit the ticket (through
the composer), your team will try again fresh.

## 4. Review — the "Needs you" queue

**"Needs you"** at the top of every page is your inbox. It's the one list of everything waiting on
your decision, oldest first. Nothing in it goes anywhere until you act.

Open an item and read it top to bottom: what you asked for, what your team says it did, and the
change itself. You don't need to understand code — read the summary, and judge it the way you'd
judge any colleague's work: *is this what I asked for?*

One badge worth knowing: **"this work has no automatic checks."** It means your venture's code
doesn't yet test itself, so nothing mechanical has verified this change — your read of it is the
only check. It's a reason to read a little more carefully, and a reason "add automatic checks" is
usually somewhere in your backlog.

## 5. Accept — or send it back

If the work is what you asked for, press **Accept**. It becomes part of your product — really:
that click is the moment the change lands.

If it isn't right, don't accept it. Go to the composer and say what's wrong ("the audit missed the
mobile views" / "this changed copy I never asked about") — that becomes the team's next
instruction. Work you ignore just waits; nothing merges itself.

## 6. Launch — see your product

Each of your surfaces (Build, Sell, Scale) has a door to the real running thing — the app, the
site — when one exists. After you accept work, open the product and look. Trust your eyes over any
summary, including your team's.

## Where everything is written down

- **Your venture's page** — the board: where things stand, what's moving, your surfaces.
- **Needs you** — decisions waiting on you.
- **What happened** — the diary of everything that shipped, newest first.
- **What your venture knows** — every decision and document you've fed it. Your team reads this
  before working, which is why a good composer conversation today makes next month's work better.

## For the curious (safe to skip)

Under the hood, your venture is ordinary, portable software infrastructure that you co-own: the
tickets are files in your venture's own repository, "accepting work" merges a pull request, and
"what your venture knows" is a folder your AI team searches before it acts. You never need to
touch any of that — but it's yours, it leaves with you, and nothing about your company is locked
inside this studio.
