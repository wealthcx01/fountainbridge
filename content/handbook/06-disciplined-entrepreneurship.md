# Chapter 6 — Disciplined Entrepreneurship

Chapters 1 through 4 taught you how to start a venture without dying the usual way. This
chapter is the map underneath them. It sets out the whole method those chapters draw on —
all of it, in order — so that when a lane in the studio does something and calls it "the
value proposition" or "the beachhead" or "COCA," you know exactly what it means and where
it sits.

The method is **Disciplined Entrepreneurship**, created by **Bill Aulet**, managing
director of the **Martin Trust Center for MIT Entrepreneurship**, and set out in his book
*Disciplined Entrepreneurship: 24 Steps to a Successful Startup* (2013). We use it because
it settles an old argument. People love to say entrepreneurship can't be taught — that it's
a gift, a knack, a matter of vibes. Aulet's answer is that the *product* of
entrepreneurship, the company, can absolutely be built by method, the same way engineering
is a method rather than a mood. The 24 steps are that method: a repeatable way to go from a
raw idea to a paying customer without betting the whole thing on luck.

Two things to fix in your head before we start.

**This is for IDE, not SME.** Aulet draws a hard line between two kinds of new business.
An **SME** — a small or medium enterprise — is a plumber, a restaurant, a local agency:
valuable, often local, usually serving a market that already exists. An **IDE** — an
**Innovation-Driven Enterprise** — is built to sell something new to a global or fast-
growing market, and it tends to lose money before it makes it, then scale hard. The 24
steps are for building an **IDE**. A Foundry venture is, by design, an IDE. If you were
opening a café, you would not need this chapter.

**There is one condition under all 24 steps.** Aulet states it as plainly as it can be
stated: **the single necessary and sufficient condition for a business is a paying
customer.** Not a product, not a technology, not a team, not funding — a person who hands
over money. Every step below exists to find that person, understand them, and earn their
money honestly. Hold that sentence up against anything you're doing; if the work isn't
moving you toward a paying customer, it's a hobby.

A word on the shape of what follows. The steps are grouped into **six questions**, and the
numbers **jump around inside each question** — you'll see Theme 1 run 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, then 9.
That's Aulet's own layout, and we keep it: the six questions are the natural order to
*think* in, and a few steps belong to one question but only make sense once you've done work
that carries a later number. Don't renumber them in your head. And the whole thing is not a
straight line — Aulet's word for the real motion is **"spiralling."** You'll answer step 9,
learn something, and go back to sharpen step 2. That's the method working, not you failing.

## Step 0 — Getting Started

Before the six questions, there's a beginning. Aulet says a venture starts from one of three
places: an **idea**, a **technology**, or a **passion**. Any of the three is a legitimate
seed — but none of them is a company yet, and the mistake is to fall in love with the seed
instead of the customer it might serve. The other half of getting started is people:
**assemble a founding team**, because the steps ahead are too much for one person and the
team you pick shapes everything you can attempt. In a Foundry venture the "team" includes the
studio's lanes — but the founder's judgement is still the thing at the centre.

---

## Theme 1 — Who is your customer?

This is the question that decides everything downstream, and it's where first-time founders
lose the most time by refusing to narrow. Chapter 1 of this handbook is this whole theme
worked through end to end (plus the start of Theme 2); here is the reference version.

**1 — Market Segmentation.** Brainstorm as widely as you can, then break the world into
distinct groups who would buy for similar reasons in similar ways. Breadth first: list a
dozen candidate markets before you fall for one. The discipline here is to segment by the
**job the customer is trying to get done**, not by demographics — "25-to-34, urban" doesn't
buy anything; a shared, urgent task does.

**2 — Select a Beachhead Market.** Choose **one** segment to win completely before you touch
any other. Aulet takes the **beachhead** idea from **Geoffrey Moore** — the bowling-alley,
lead-pin image: you knock down one pin so it knocks down the next. Think of a D-Day landing:
you don't invade the whole coast, you take one beach, own it, and push inland from strength.
A beachhead's job is to be *winnable*, not big. Scale is later.

**3 — Build an End User Profile.** Describe the specific person who will actually *use* the
product — which is often not the person who pays. This is a **composite**: a rich, recognisable
portrait of the *type* of user (what they're trying to achieve, what they fear, where they
gather), assembled from real people but not itself any single person. Keep that word,
composite — it's what separates this step from the next.

**4 — Calculate the TAM for the Beachhead Market.** Size the beachhead's **Total Addressable
Market**: the annual revenue you'd earn at **100% market share** — dollars per year, not a
count of customers. Do it **bottom-up first** — Aulet calls this **"counting noses"**: start
from countable real users and multiply by what each would pay per year. A top-down slice of
some industry-report billions is worth a sanity-check at most; the number that means anything
is the one you built from actual people. A healthy beachhead TAM is meaningful but conquerable
— roughly \$20M–\$100M a year.

**5 — Profile the Persona for the Beachhead Market.** Now collapse the End User Profile into
**one real, named, specific person** — the single human who best embodies the profile. This is
**not** the composite from step 3, and conflating the two is the most common way to misread the
whole framework: **the End User Profile is the type; the Persona is the person.** There is only
one Persona, you pin them to the wall, and you write down their **priorities in order** — the
thing that gets them praised or fired. Everything the team builds gets aimed at them.

**9 — Identify Your Next 10 Customers.** Beyond the one Persona, find **ten more** real
prospects who fit the profile, and go talk to them. This lives in Theme 1 because it's a
reality check on your customer definition — if you can't name ten reachable people who light
up, the beachhead is wrong and you go back. Stay in **inquiry mode, not advocacy mode**: you're
there to learn, not to sell. Welcome the ones who tell you why it won't work; they're a gift.

## Theme 2 — What can you do for your customer?

You know who they are. Now define what you'll do for them — concretely enough to build, and
valuable enough that they'll change their behaviour to get it.

**6 — Full Life Cycle Use Case.** Map the customer's **entire** experience with the product, not
just "they use the app." Aulet's expanded version covers everything: how they first realise they
have the problem, how they hear about you, how they weigh you up, buy, get set up, use it, decide
it was worth it, pay, get help, and tell someone else. Do it in the customer's shoes and map their
*current* way of doing things first, so you can slot into their day rather than demand they tear
it up.

**7 — High-Level Product Specification.** Only now describe the product — and even here you
**don't build it.** This is, in Aulet's words, essentially a drawing: a storyboard or wireframe, a
benefit-led picture of what the thing does for the Persona. The drawing forces everyone to agree on
what the product actually is before anyone spends money or falls in love with code.

**8 — Quantify the Value Proposition.** Turn the spec into a single number the Persona would
recognise, tied to their top priority. The mechanism is **"as-is versus possible"**: their world
today, versus their world with you, measured in *their* units. Not "our software is powerful" —
instead, the current cost of the problem against the improvement you deliver, stated as a figure.
And **under-promise**: quote a number you're confident you can beat.

**10 — Define Your Core.** Name the **one** thing you will do better than anyone, that is hard to
copy — your source of enduring advantage. The Core is **not** your competitive position (that's the
next step) and not a feature; it's the root reason you'll keep winning after competitors notice you.
It's the seed of a **Power** — the durable moat this handbook covers in the moats section. Choosing
it early tells the studio what to protect and deepen versus what to treat as commodity.

**11 — Chart Your Competitive Position.** Plot yourself against the alternatives on the **two axes
the Persona cares most about** (from their priorities in step 5) — and aim to own the top-right
corner incumbents leave empty. The honesty test: pick the axes the *customer* actually buys on, not
the ones where you happen to look good. If your Core is right, you should dominate on the Persona's
two priorities; if you can't, the value prop or the beachhead needs rework.

## Theme 3 — How does your customer acquire your product?

Wanting the product and *acquiring* it are different things. This question maps the machinery of a
purchase — who's involved and what the steps really are.

**12 — Determine the Customer's Decision-Making Unit (DMU).** Identify everyone involved in the
buying decision: the end user, the **economic buyer** who controls the money, the **champion** who
pushes for you internally, and any influencers, gatekeepers, or people with a veto. The user and the
payer are often different people, and a third party is frequently the trusted introducer — name them
all, because the enthusiastic user can't save you if a quiet veto kills the deal.

**13 — Map the Process to Acquire a Paying Customer.** Trace every step from first awareness to paid
— cradle to grave — including budget authority, the length of the sales cycle, and any regulatory or
approval hurdles. The point is to see the real process, with its gatekeepers and three-week gaps,
instead of the clean funnel you'd like to imagine.

**18 — Map the Sales Process to Acquire a Customer.** This step sits in Theme 3 conceptually but
carries a later number because it depends on the money work in Theme 4. Design how you'll actually
sell across the venture's whole life: the **short-term** motion (founder-led, high-touch and
expensive on purpose, because that's how you learn), the **medium-term**, and the **long-term**
(self-serve or channel). Early sales *should* be manual and costly; assuming the cheap scalable
channel works on day one is how founders fool themselves.

## Theme 4 — How do you make money off your product?

A product that helps people but can't capture value is a hobby. This question builds the economic
engine — and ends on the one comparison that decides whether you have a business at all.

**15 — Design a Business Model.** Choose *how* you capture value — subscription, usage, licensing,
transaction, freemium — matched to how the customer actually receives and perceives value. The model
is a strategic choice, not a price. Ask whether the customer feels value continuously or just once
before you default to "we'll charge a subscription."

**16 — Set Your Pricing Framework.** Price against the **quantified value** from step 8 and the
customer's real alternatives — capturing a fair fraction of the value you create, **not cost-plus**.
Cost-plus pricing ignores value entirely and usually leaves most of it on the table. Keep the price
legible; consider the economics for early customers and champions.

**17 — Calculate the Lifetime Value (LTV) of an Acquired Customer.** The total profit from an
average customer over the whole relationship — built from revenue, margin, retention, and expansion,
discounted for time. **LTV is the ceiling on what you can afford to spend to win a customer.** Retention
is the biggest lever, which is why the boring middle — onboarding and habit — is an economic decision,
not just a design one. Don't inflate it with a fantasy churn rate.

**19 — Calculate the Cost of Customer Acquisition (COCA).** The fully-loaded cost to acquire one
customer over time — all sales and marketing spend divided by customers won — computed **top-down**,
and crucially, how it *falls* as the motion matures. Note the term: Aulet says **COCA**, not the
industry's "CAC," to keep you honest about what's really in the number. Then compare it to LTV. The
venture-killer is **COCA ≥ LTV**: if it costs more to win a customer than they're worth, no amount of
growth saves you — you just lose money faster. Fix the model before you scale it.

---

## Theme 5 — How do you design and build your product?

Everything so far is a well-reasoned hypothesis. This question turns it into evidence — cheaply,
before you over-build.

**20 — Identify Key Assumptions.** Extract the specific beliefs your plan depends on — the ones that
would sink the venture if they're wrong — and separate them from what you already know. Rank them by
"how fatal if false" against "how unsure am I," and attack the top of that list first.

**21 — Test Key Assumptions.** Design the **cheapest experiment that could disprove** each assumption,
and run it — landing pages, opt-in tests, concierge prototypes, fake-door tests. The goal is truth
fast, not confirmation. If a test can only come back "yes," it isn't a test.

**22 — Define the Minimum Viable Business Product (MVBP).** The smallest thing that (a) delivers real
value, (b) the customer actually **pays** for, and (c) starts your feedback loop. The middle word is
the point. Aulet builds this on Eric Ries's and Steve Blank's lean thinking but pointedly **modifies
the MVP into the MVBP** — a Minimum Viable *Business* Product — because an MVP can be a demo that
proves nothing, while an MVBP must **transact**. If no money changes hands, you haven't tested the
business.

**23 — Show That "The Dogs Will Eat the Dog Food."** Prove real customers adopt, use, and keep paying
— behaviour, not surveys. The metaphor is blunt on purpose: it doesn't matter how good the food is if
the dogs won't eat it. Watch the cohort — activation, week-four retention, renewal. Sustained use and
repeat payment is the proof; sign-ups are easy to fake, and praise proves nothing.

---

## Theme 6 — How do you scale your business?

The last question only earns its place once the first five are answered with evidence. Scaling is what
you do *after* the dogs eat the dog food.

**14 — Calculate the TAM Size for Follow-on Markets.** This carries an early number but belongs to
scaling: estimate the *additional* markets you could enter after winning the beachhead — adjacent
segments, or new offerings to the same customers, ideally sharing the Core you named in step 10. Sketch
it to prove the beachhead is a doorway and not a dead end — but **do not work these markets now.** They
are a map for later; the beachhead is the only battle you're actually fighting.

**24 — Develop a Product Plan.** Lay out how the product evolves beyond the MVBP: the staged sequence
of markets and capabilities that carries you from the beachhead into the adjacent opportunities. It
stays a *plan* — you advance to the next market only when the current one is genuinely won on retention
and unit economics, not hope. Each new market re-runs the earlier questions in miniature; it doesn't
get to skip them. Scaling on vanity, before the beachhead's retention holds, just multiplies the leak.

## How to hold all this

Two closing points, because the list can read as more rigid than the method is.

**It spirals.** The 24 steps are not a checklist you complete top to bottom once. You'll get to step 9
and discover a better Persona than the one you started with; you'll size the TAM at step 4 and realise
the beachhead is too small; you'll test an assumption at step 21 and rewrite the value proposition at
step 8. Aulet designed it to be walked more than once. The studio keeps every version of your thinking
so you can see how it moved.

**Aulet stands on other people's shoulders, and says so.** The beachhead comes from **Geoffrey
Moore**; the "own the empty corner" instinct behind competitive positioning echoes **Kim and
Mauborgne's Blue Ocean** thinking; the build-measure-learn spirit of Theme 5 comes from **Steve Blank
and Eric Ries**, which Aulet modifies into the MVBP. Disciplined Entrepreneurship is the synthesis — a
single ordered method that pulls these threads into 24 concrete steps.

**And you have already met most of it.** Chapters 1 through 4 of this handbook *are* this framework,
applied to how a Foundry venture is actually built. Chapter 1 walked Theme 1 and the front of Theme 2 —
segmentation, the beachhead, End User Profile and Persona, bottom-up TAM, the life-cycle map, the
drawing, the value number, and the next ten customers. Chapters 2 through 4 carried the rest into
practice: build the smallest thing they'll pay for, sell it the way people want to be sold to, and grow
it without breaking it. This chapter is where the full detail lives — the reference you come back to when
a lane names a step and you want to know precisely what it's doing and why.

## What this chapter gives you

- A shared vocabulary: when the studio says **beachhead**, **End User Profile** versus **Persona**,
  **TAM**, **Core**, **quantified value proposition**, **MVBP**, **LTV**, or **COCA**, you know exactly
  what's meant — and where each sits among the six questions.
- The **one condition** under all of it: a paying customer. If the work isn't moving toward one, stop
  and ask why.
- The distinction that founders most often blur — **End User Profile (the composite type) versus
  Persona (the one real person)** — stated the way Aulet means it.
- A frame for scope: **IDE, not SME.** A Foundry venture is an Innovation-Driven Enterprise, and these
  24 steps are built for exactly that.
- Permission to spiral. Going back to sharpen an earlier step is the method working, not a setback.
