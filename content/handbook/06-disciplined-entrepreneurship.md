# Chapter 6 — Disciplined Entrepreneurship

Chapters 1 through 4 taught you how to start a venture without dying the usual way. This
chapter is the map underneath them. It sets out the whole method those chapters draw on —
all of it, in order — so that when a lane in the studio does something and calls it "the
value proposition" or "the beachhead" or "COCA," you know exactly what it means and where
it sits.

The method is **Disciplined Entrepreneurship**, created by **Bill Aulet**, managing
director of the **Martin Trust Center for MIT Entrepreneurship**, and set out in his book
*Disciplined Entrepreneurship: 24 Steps to a Successful Startup* (2013). We use it because
it settles an old argument. People love to say entrepreneurship can't be taught — that it's
a gift, a knack, a matter of vibes. Aulet's answer is that the *product* of
entrepreneurship, the company, can absolutely be built by method, the same way engineering
is a method rather than a mood. The 24 steps are that method: a repeatable way to go from a
raw idea to a paying customer without betting the whole thing on luck.

Two things to fix in your head before we start.

**This is for IDE, not SME.** Aulet draws a hard line between two kinds of new business.
An **SME** — a small or medium enterprise — is a plumber, a restaurant, a local agency:
valuable, often local, usually serving a market that already exists. An **IDE** — an
**Innovation-Driven Enterprise** — is built to sell something new to a global or fast-
growing market, and it tends to lose money before it makes it, then scale hard. The 24
steps are for building an **IDE**. A Foundry venture is, by design, an IDE. If you were
opening a café, you would not need this chapter.

**There is one condition under all 24 steps.** Aulet states it as plainly as it can be
stated: **the single necessary and sufficient condition for a business is a paying
customer.** Not a product, not a technology, not a team, not funding — a person who hands
over money. Every step below exists to find that person, understand them, and earn their
money honestly. Hold that sentence up against anything you're doing; if the work isn't
moving you toward a paying customer, it's a hobby.

A word on the shape of what follows. The steps are grouped into **six questions**, and the
numbers **jump around inside each question** — you'll see Theme 1 run 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, then 9.
That's Aulet's own layout, and we keep it: the six questions are the natural order to
*think* in, and a few steps belong to one question but only make sense once you've done work
that carries a later number. Don't renumber them in your head. And the whole thing is not a
straight line — Aulet's word for the real motion is **"spiralling."** You'll answer step 9,
learn something, and go back to sharpen step 2. That's the method working, not you failing.

A note on how the steps are laid out below. Each one gets the same four-part treatment, so
you can read it as a working lesson rather than a definition: **what it means**, **for a
Foundry venture** (how the studio and its lanes actually run the step), a concrete
**example** — we use one running example throughout, an independent dental practice losing
money to missed appointments, so the ideas stay grounded — and the **trap**, the specific
way first-time founders get this step wrong.

The order matters more than anything. Most ventures die because they answer question four
(how do you make money) before question one (who is your customer). Resist that.

## Step 0 — Getting Started

Before the six questions, there's a beginning. Aulet says a venture starts from one of three
places: an **idea**, a **technology**, or a **passion**. Any of the three is a legitimate
seed — but none of them is a company yet, and the mistake is to fall in love with the seed
instead of the customer it might serve. The other half of getting started is people:
**assemble a founding team**, because the steps ahead are too much for one person and the
team you pick shapes everything you can attempt. In a Foundry venture the "team" includes the
studio's lanes — but the founder's judgement is still the thing at the centre.

## Theme 1 — Who is your customer?

This is the question that decides everything downstream, and it's where first-time founders
lose the most time by refusing to narrow. You cannot build for everyone, so stop trying —
narrow until it hurts. A Foundry venture doesn't get to "everyone with a problem"; it earns
one specific group of people who feel one specific pain, and grows out from there. Because
our go-to-market is interest-based — no cold outreach, we reach people who've signalled they
want the thing — a sharp customer definition isn't optional. You can't attract interest from
a market you haven't named. Chapter 1 of this handbook is this whole theme worked through
end to end (plus the start of Theme 2); here is the reference version.

**1 — Market Segmentation.**
*What it means.* Brainstorm as widely as you can, then break the world into distinct groups
who would buy for similar reasons in similar ways. Breadth first: list a dozen candidate
markets before you fall for one, so you don't marry the first idea you had in the shower.
The discipline is to segment by the **job the customer is trying to get done**, not by
demographics — "25-to-34, urban" doesn't buy anything; a shared, urgent task does.
*For a Foundry venture.* Run this in a lane as a research pass: enumerate six to twelve
candidate segments, each with the end user (the actual human, not the organisation), the
task they're trying to get done, and why now. It lands in the venture repo as a working
document every later lane reads.
*Example.* "Help dental practices lose less money to no-shows" segments into: the front-desk
coordinator at a small private practice, the office manager at a multi-site group, the
principal dentist at a mixed NHS practice, specialist orthodontic clinics with long
treatment plans, and dental-software resellers who'd bundle it — each a different buyer, a
different rhythm, a different reason to care.
*Trap.* Segmenting by demographics or by organisation ("dental practices," "SMEs") instead
of by job-to-be-done and the human who does it. A practice doesn't chase a no-show; a person
at the front desk does.

**2 — Select a Beachhead Market.**
*What it means.* Choose **one** segment to win completely before you touch any other. Aulet
takes the **beachhead** idea from **Geoffrey Moore** — the bowling-alley, lead-pin image:
you knock down one pin so it knocks down the next. Think of a D-Day landing: you don't invade
the whole coast, you take one beach, own it, and push inland from strength. A beachhead's job
is to be *winnable*, not big — small enough to dominate, real enough to matter, reachable
without a miracle. Scale is later.
*For a Foundry venture.* Pick the beachhead where interest is easiest to earn honestly — an
existing community, list, or channel where these people already gather and would welcome the
thing. Consent-first GTM makes "where do they already congregate?" a selection criterion,
not an afterthought.
*Example.* Of the dental segments, "the front-desk coordinator at two-to-four-chair private
practices in one region" — a bounded, reachable group with an acute, repeating pain (every
empty chair is money) who talk to each other at the same conferences and in the same Facebook
groups.
*Trap.* Picking a beachhead because it's big. A beachhead's job is to be winnable; scale
comes later. Aulet's line, and ours: he has yet to see a founder focus too much — it is
always the other way around.

**3 — Build an End User Profile.**
*What it means.* Describe the specific person who will actually *use* the product — which is
often not the person who pays. This is a **composite**: a rich, recognisable portrait of the
*type* of user (what they're trying to achieve, what they fear, who their hero is, what they
read, where they gather — Aulet's "watering holes"), assembled from real people but not itself
any single person. Keep that word, composite — it's what separates this step from the next.
*For a Foundry venture.* Write it down in the venture repo so the lanes and the studio
reference it — every later decision (copy, features, sends) then points at the same person
instead of drifting to a generic "user."
*Example.* "A coordinator, 30s, runs the front desk single-handed, dreads the owner asking
why revenue dipped, lives in the practice-management software all day, chases no-shows with a
phone call and a sticky note, reads a couple of dental-admin Facebook groups."
*Trap.* A profile so broad that two people who both fit it would want opposite things. If it
doesn't narrow the room, it isn't a profile.

**4 — Calculate the TAM for the Beachhead Market.**
*What it means.* Size the beachhead's **Total Addressable Market**: the annual revenue you'd
earn at **100% market share** — dollars per year, not a count of customers. Do it
**bottom-up first** — Aulet calls this **"counting noses"**: start from countable real users
and multiply by what each would realistically pay per year. A top-down slice of some
industry-report billions is worth a sanity-check at most; the number that means anything is
the one you built from actual people, because you never find a real live customer hiding in a
cell on a spreadsheet.
*For a Foundry venture.* Aim for a beachhead TAM that's meaningful but conquerable — Aulet's
rule of thumb is roughly £15–75m a year: big enough to build a real business on, small enough
to own. Defend it line by line in the repo.
*Example.* Number of independent two-to-four-chair practices in your first region × the
annual value of the no-shows you'd help them recover → a bottom-up figure built from a real,
countable population, not "1% of the global dental-software market."
*Trap.* "1% of a £10B market." Top-down percentages are wishes, not markets — they always
flatter you, and they hide the fact that you haven't counted a single nose.

**5 — Profile the Persona for the Beachhead Market.**
*What it means.* Now collapse the End User Profile into **one real, named, specific person** —
the single human who best embodies the profile, with their priorities, watering holes, and
buying criteria. This is **not** the composite from step 3, and conflating the two is the most
common way to misread the whole framework: **the End User Profile is the type; the Persona is
the person.** There is only one Persona, you pin them to the wall, and you write down their
**priorities in order** — the thing that gets them praised or fired. Treat what they *say*
with care and watch what they *do*: people will tell you they care about the environment and
then buy on price every time. Everything the team builds gets aimed at this one person.
*For a Foundry venture.* The Persona is the audience for every interest-based send. If you
can't name where they already pay attention, you can't reach them on consent — so the Persona
isn't a poster, it's an addressing scheme.
*Example.* "Sam, 34, front-desk coordinator at a three-chair private practice; wants to stop
being the person who has to explain a bad month to the principal; lives in the diary software;
in two dental-admin Facebook groups." Every send is written to Sam.
*Trap.* Inventing a Persona from your imagination instead of grounding it in primary
conversations with real people. A made-up Persona will happily agree with whatever you already
wanted to build.

**9 — Identify Your Next 10 Customers.**
*What it means.* Beyond the one Persona, name **ten more** real prospects who fit the profile
and would plausibly buy — a reality check that the beachhead isn't a party of one — and go
talk to them. This lives in Theme 1 because it tests your customer definition: if you can't
name ten reachable, consenting people who light up, the beachhead (step 2) is wrong and you go
back. Stay in **inquiry mode, not advocacy mode**: you're there to learn, not to sell. Welcome
the ones who tell you why it won't work; they're a gift.
*For a Foundry venture.* These ten are your first interest-based conversations. They're not a
list you hope exists — they're ten people you could email today, gathered lawfully, each a
real name attached to a real practice.
*Example.* Ten named coordinators — reached through a dental-admin group or two partner
suppliers — who've agreed to talk, not "people like Sam" in the abstract.
*Trap.* Listing friends and well-wishers who'll be nice to you instead of buyers who'll be
honest. A polite yes from someone who'll never pay is worse than a blunt no from someone who
would.

## Theme 2 — What can you do for your customer?

You know who they are. Now define what you'll do for them — concretely enough to build, and
valuable enough that they'll change their behaviour to get it. Value is not features; it is a
**measurable change in the customer's world**. This is where a vague "app for dentists"
becomes a specific promise with a number attached.

**6 — Full Life Cycle Use Case.**
*What it means.* Map the customer's **entire** experience with the product, not just "they use
the app." Aulet's expanded version covers everything: how they first realise they have the
problem, how they hear about you, how they weigh you up, buy, get set up, use it, decide it was
worth it, pay, get help, and tell someone else. Do it in the customer's shoes and map their
*current* way of doing things first, so you can slot into their day rather than demand they
tear it up.
*For a Foundry venture.* This map exposes every point where an external action fires — a send,
a sign-up, a payment — which are exactly the points that pass through the approval gate. Design
the journey and the gates together, not as two separate exercises.
*Example.* Coordinator hears about you in a dental-admin group → sees an opt-in message → tries
a free first month → the tool auto-nudges patients before appointments → she sees the empty-chair
count drop → she subscribes → she recommends it in the group. Each arrow is a step you can
instrument.
*Trap.* Designing the flashy acquisition moment and ignoring the boring middle — onboarding and
habit — where ventures actually die. Your coordinator already chases no-shows *somehow* today;
if you don't obviously beat the sticky note in week one, you lose.

**7 — High-Level Product Specification.**
*What it means.* Only now describe the product — and even here you **don't build it**. This is,
in Aulet's words, essentially a drawing: a storyboard or wireframe, a benefit-led picture of
what the thing does for the Persona. It's a brochure, not a blueprint. The drawing forces
everyone to agree on what the product actually is before anyone spends money or falls in love
with code.
*For a Foundry venture.* Draft it in the repo as the shared reference the lanes build against;
it's what later turns into tickets. Keep it benefit-first ("the principal never has to ask why
revenue dipped"), not feature-first.
*Example.* Three screens: an at-a-glance "chairs at risk this week," an automatic reminder that
goes out without the coordinator lifting a finger, and a simple "money recovered" number she can
show the principal — shown as pictures, not a database schema.
*Trap.* Speccing features you find clever instead of the outcome the Persona is hiring you for.
Nobody buys "SMS integration"; they buy "I stopped losing £150 a chair."

**8 — Quantify the Value Proposition.**
*What it means.* Turn the spec into a single number the Persona would recognise, tied to their
top priority (from step 5). The mechanism is **"as-is versus possible"**: their world today,
versus their world with you, measured in *their* units. Not "our software is powerful" — instead
the current cost of the problem against the improvement you deliver, stated as a figure. And
**under-promise**: quote a number you're confident you can beat.
*For a Foundry venture.* This single quantified claim becomes the spine of all interest-based
copy — honest, specific, measurable — and it's also what you'll later have to prove.
*Example.* "Recover eight empty chairs a month — about £1,200 you're currently losing" beats
"increase efficiency." It's tied to the coordinator's top priority (don't be the reason revenue
dipped), not a generic "run smoother."
*Trap.* Value stated as features ("automatic reminders") rather than the gain the customer feels
("stop losing £1,200 a month"). If you can't put a number on it, you're guessing at value.

**10 — Define Your Core.**
*What it means.* Name the **one** thing you will do better than anyone, that is hard to copy —
your source of enduring advantage. The Core is **not** your competitive position (that's the next
step) and not a feature; it's the root reason you'll keep winning after competitors notice you.
Everything else you can buy, borrow, or defer. It's the seed of a **Power** — the durable moat
this handbook covers in Chapter 7.
*For a Foundry venture.* Choosing the Core early tells the lanes what to protect and deepen versus
what to treat as commodity. It usually starts life as the "unique insight" you wrote down when you
pressure-tested the idea in Chapter 1.
*Example.* A proprietary dataset of which reminder, sent when, actually gets which kind of patient
to show up — accumulated across every practice you serve, making your nudges measurably better the
more practices use them. A competitor can copy the screens; they can't copy the data.
*Trap.* Claiming your brand or your team as the Core on day one, before either is actually rare. A
Core has to be something a competitor genuinely can't get, not something you hope to be good at.

**11 — Chart Your Competitive Position.**
*What it means.* Plot yourself against the alternatives on the **two axes the Persona cares most
about** (from their priorities in step 5) — and aim to own the top-right corner incumbents leave
empty. The honesty test: pick the axes the *customer* actually buys on, not the ones where you
happen to look good.
*For a Foundry venture.* If your Core is right, you should dominate on the Persona's two priorities.
If you can't, the value prop or the beachhead needs rework — better to learn it here, on a chart,
than after launch.
*Example.* Axes "recovers money automatically" × "no work for the front desk" — a quadrant that
generic practice-management suites (feature-heavy, effortful) and the manual phone-call-and-sticky-note
status quo (effortful, leaky) both leave empty.
*Trap.* Choosing axes where you look good instead of the axes the customer actually buys on. Winning
a chart nobody buys on is losing.

## Theme 3 — How does your customer acquire your product?

Wanting the product and *acquiring* it are different things. This question maps the machinery of a
purchase — who's involved and what the steps really are. For a consent-first venture it's also where
"how they acquire it" and "how we're allowed to reach them" have to line up.

**12 — Determine the Customer's Decision-Making Unit (DMU).**
*What it means.* Identify everyone involved in the buying decision: the end user, the **economic
buyer** who controls the money, the **champion** who pushes for you internally, and any influencers,
gatekeepers, or people with a veto. The user and the payer are often different people, and a third
party is frequently the trusted introducer — name them all, because the enthusiastic user can't save
you if a quiet veto kills the deal.
*For a Foundry venture.* Interest-based GTM lives or dies on the introducer's consent, so name that
third party explicitly — they're often how you reach the beachhead lawfully in the first place.
*Example.* User = the front-desk coordinator; champion = the coordinator (or the practice manager);
economic buyer = the principal dentist who signs off spend; gatekeeper = whoever guards patient-data
policy. Delight the coordinator all you like — if the principal won't sign, nothing happens.
*Trap.* Selling only to the enthusiastic user while ignoring the economic buyer or the veto who
quietly kills it. The person who loves you and the person who pays are rarely the same person.

**13 — Map the Process to Acquire a Paying Customer.**
*What it means.* Trace every step from first awareness to paid — cradle to grave — including budget
authority, the length of the sales cycle, and any regulatory or approval hurdles. The point is to see
the real process, with its gatekeepers and three-week gaps, instead of the clean funnel you'd like to
imagine.
*For a Foundry venture.* Every outbound touch in this map is an external action that stops at the
approval gate before it fires. Mapping the process is therefore also mapping where human consent is
required — design the two as one thing, not two.
*Example.* Group post the coordinator opts into → welcome message → free month → conversion message →
the principal signs off → checkout → a progress number the principal can see. Each message is drafted
by a lane and released only on approval.
*Trap.* Assuming a short, clean funnel when the real process has a gatekeeper, a data-policy check, and
a three-week gap while the principal is on holiday that you didn't budget for.

**18 — Map the Sales Process to Acquire a Customer.**
*What it means.* This step sits in Theme 3 conceptually but carries a later number because it depends on
the money work in Theme 4. Design how you'll actually sell across the venture's whole life: the
**short-term** motion (founder-led, high-touch and expensive on purpose, because that's how you learn),
the **medium-term**, and the **long-term** (self-serve or channel) — and the cost of each. Early sales
*should* be manual and costly; assuming the cheap scalable channel works on day one is how founders fool
themselves.
*For a Foundry venture.* Early on the sales process is largely approved sends and honest conversations;
later it's more automated. Each stage still ends at the same gate — nothing ships to a human without a
yes.
*Example.* Phase 1: founder plus a couple of champion practices, hand-approved messages. Phase 2:
templated opt-in journeys. Phase 3: a self-serve sign-up a practice can complete alone.
*Trap.* Assuming the cheap, scalable channel works from day one. Early sales are expensive and manual on
purpose — that expense is the tuition you pay to learn how the sale really works.

## Theme 4 — How do you make money off your product?

A product that helps people but can't capture value is a hobby. This question builds the economic engine —
how you charge, what a customer is worth, what it costs to win one — and ends on the one comparison that
decides whether you have a business at all.

**15 — Design a Business Model.**
*What it means.* Choose *how* you capture value — subscription, usage, licensing, transaction, freemium —
matched to how the customer actually receives and perceives value. The model is a strategic choice, not a
price. Ask whether the customer feels value continuously or just once before you default to "we'll charge a
subscription."
*For a Foundry venture.* Prefer models that compound with the interest-based, consent-first motion: recurring
value → recurring revenue, with the introducer's trust intact.
*Example.* A monthly subscription per practice for the automated reminders makes sense because the value —
fewer empty chairs — recurs every single week. A one-off setup fee would misread how the value actually
arrives.
*Trap.* Defaulting to "we'll charge a subscription" without asking whether the customer experiences value
continuously or just once. The model should mirror the value's rhythm, not your cash-flow wish.

**16 — Set Your Pricing Framework.**
*What it means.* Price against the **quantified value** from step 8 and the customer's real alternatives —
capturing a fair fraction of the value you create, **not cost-plus**. Cost-plus pricing ignores value
entirely and usually leaves most of it on the table. Keep the price legible, and consider the economics for
early customers and champions.
*For a Foundry venture.* Anchor the price to the value number you can defend, and keep it legible; opaque
pricing corrodes the trust that consent-based GTM runs on.
*Example.* Priced as a fraction of "about £1,200 of recovered chairs a month" — say a clear monthly fee well
under that — a number the coordinator accepts without needing a spreadsheet.
*Trap.* Cost-plus pricing ("it costs us £X to run, so we'll charge £X plus a margin"), which ignores value
entirely and usually leaves most of it on the table.

**17 — Calculate the Lifetime Value (LTV) of an Acquired Customer.**
*What it means.* The total profit from an average customer over the whole relationship — built from revenue,
margin, retention, and expansion, discounted for time. **LTV is the ceiling on what you can afford to spend to
win a customer.** Retention is the biggest lever, which is why the boring middle — onboarding and habit — is an
economic decision, not just a design one.
*For a Foundry venture.* Because retention dominates LTV, the "boring middle" (getting the coordinator into the
habit) is a number on this line, not a UX nicety.
*Example.* Monthly subscription margin × the expected number of months a practice stays + any expansion (extra
locations, add-ons) → a defensible per-customer LTV you built from evidence.
*Trap.* Inflating LTV with a fantasy retention curve. A hopeful churn rate hides a broken model; use the
retention you can actually observe, not the one you'd like.

**19 — Calculate the Cost of Customer Acquisition (COCA).**
*What it means.* The fully-loaded cost to acquire one customer over time — all sales and marketing spend divided
by customers won — computed **top-down**, and crucially, how it *falls* as the motion matures. Note the term:
Aulet says **COCA**, not the industry's "CAC," to keep you honest about what's really in the number (the whole
loaded cost of the sales and marketing machine, not just the ad click). Then compare it to LTV.
*For a Foundry venture.* Interest-based acquisition should drive COCA *down* structurally — reaching people who
already want the thing is cheaper than interrupting people who don't. Proving that is proving the engine works.
*Example.* High COCA in the founder-led phase, dropping sharply as champion practices refer others and opt-in
journeys compound; you plot the curve, not a single point.
*Trap.* The venture-killer: **COCA ≥ LTV.** If it costs more to win a customer than they're worth, no amount of
growth saves you — you just lose money faster. Fix the model before you scale it.

## Theme 5 — How do you design and build your product?

Everything so far is a well-reasoned hypothesis. This question turns it into evidence — cheaply, before you
over-build. It's also where Foundry's speed advantage is real: because the lanes can build and revise fast behind
the gate, you can run more honest experiments per week than a traditional team — as long as you're testing the
risky things and not polishing the safe ones.

**20 — Identify Key Assumptions.**
*What it means.* Extract the specific beliefs your plan depends on — the ones that would sink the venture if
they're wrong — and separate them from what you already know. Rank them by "how fatal if false" against "how
unsure am I," and attack the top of that list first.
*For a Foundry venture.* Write them as a list in the repo; each becomes a testable ticket, sequenced by that
fatal-times-unsure ranking.
*Example.* "Coordinators will let an outside tool message their patients," "patients actually show up more when
nudged," "the principal will pay monthly for recovered chairs" — each a load-bearing beam that, if rotten, brings
the house down.
*Trap.* Testing the assumptions that are easy or flattering to test instead of the ones that would hurt most if
false. Comfortable experiments are procrastination in a lab coat.

**21 — Test Key Assumptions.**
*What it means.* Design the **cheapest experiment that could disprove** each assumption, and run it — landing
pages, opt-in tests, concierge prototypes, fake-door tests. The goal is truth fast, not confirmation. If a test
can only come back "yes," it isn't a test.
*For a Foundry venture.* Lanes stand these up quickly — but any experiment that touches a real person (a send, a
sign-up) still passes the approval gate. Fast to build, still consent-first to release.
*Example.* One approved opt-in message to a single dental-admin group: do coordinators actually click through to
start a free month? One real signal beats a month of opinion.
*Trap.* Running experiments that can only succeed. If a test can't come back "no," it isn't a test — it's a
demo you're using to reassure yourself.

**22 — Define the Minimum Viable Business Product (MVBP).**
*What it means.* The smallest thing that (a) delivers real value, (b) the customer actually **pays** for, and
(c) starts your feedback loop. The middle word is the point. Aulet builds this on Eric Ries's and Steve Blank's
lean thinking but pointedly **modifies the MVP into the MVBP** — a Minimum Viable *Business* Product — because an
MVP can be a demo that proves nothing, while an MVBP must **transact**. If no money changes hands, you haven't
tested the business.
*For a Foundry venture.* Scope the MVBP as a tight set of tickets aimed only at the value prop (step 8). Everything
not required to deliver and charge for that value is deferred to a follow-up ticket.
*Example.* One automated reminder flow, a paywall after the free month, and a "money recovered" number the
principal can see — nothing else, but a real paid loop that actually takes a card.
*Trap.* Confusing an MVP demo with an MVBP. If no money changes hands, you haven't tested the business — you've
tested whether people like a mock-up, which they always do.

**23 — Show That "The Dogs Will Eat the Dog Food."**
*What it means.* Prove real customers adopt, use, and keep paying — behaviour, not surveys. The metaphor is blunt
on purpose: it doesn't matter how good the food is if the dogs won't eat it. Watch the cohort — activation, week-four
retention, renewal. Sustained use and repeat payment is the proof; sign-ups are easy to fake, and praise proves
nothing.
*For a Foundry venture.* Instrument the full life-cycle use case (step 6) and watch the cohort in the studio's
activity and health feeds — real usage, made visible, not anecdote.
*Example.* Of the first cohort of practices, do they still have the reminders running in week four, and do they
renew? Sustained use and repeat payment is the proof; a warm testimonial isn't.
*Trap.* Declaring victory on sign-ups. Acquisition is easy to fake; retention is the honest signal. A full sign-up
funnel over a leaking retention bucket is a slow, expensive way to fail.

## Theme 6 — How do you scale your business?

The last question only earns its place once the first five are answered with evidence. Scaling is what you do *after*
the dogs eat the dog food — expanding deliberately from a beachhead you actually own. Scale a broken model and you
just lose money faster; scale a proven one and the earlier discipline compounds.

**14 — Calculate the TAM Size for Follow-on Markets.**
*What it means.* This carries an early number but belongs to scaling: estimate the *additional* markets you could
enter after winning the beachhead — adjacent segments, or new offerings to the same customers, ideally sharing the
Core you named in step 10. It's the bridge to the scaling question.
*For a Foundry venture.* Sketch it to prove the beachhead is a doorway and not a dead end — but **do not let
follow-on markets pull focus.** They exist to show the upside and sequence later work, nothing more yet.
*Example.* From small private-practice no-shows → multi-site dental groups → adjacent appointment-heavy clinics
(physio, optometry) → practice-side analytics built on the same reminder dataset (the Core) — widening markets, one
shared engine.
*Trap.* Working the follow-on markets now. They're a map for later; the beachhead is the only battle you're actually
fighting, and every hour spent daydreaming about market three is an hour stolen from winning market one.

**24 — Develop a Product Plan.**
*What it means.* Lay out how the product evolves beyond the MVBP: the staged sequence of markets and capabilities that
carries you from the beachhead into the adjacent opportunities, prioritised and staged. It stays a *plan* — you advance
to the next market only when the current one is genuinely won on retention and unit economics, not hope.
*For a Foundry venture.* The plan becomes a roadmap of tickets across lanes — but it stays a plan. Each new market
re-runs the earlier questions in miniature; it doesn't get to skip them.
*Example.* Beachhead: small-practice no-shows, proven on retention. Next: multi-site groups (same Core dataset, new
buyer) → adjacent clinics → practice-side analytics — each entered only after the prior cohort holds.
*Trap.* Scaling on vanity. Adding markets or features while the beachhead's retention is unproven multiplies the leak
instead of the value — premature scaling is the classic way a promising venture dies.

## How to hold all this

Two closing points, because the list can read as more rigid than the method is.

**It spirals.** The 24 steps are not a checklist you complete top to bottom once. You'll get to step 9
and discover a better Persona than the one you started with; you'll size the TAM at step 4 and realise
the beachhead is too small; you'll test an assumption at step 21 and rewrite the value proposition at
step 8. Aulet designed it to be walked more than once. The studio keeps every version of your thinking
so you can see how it moved.

**Aulet stands on other people's shoulders, and says so.** The beachhead comes from **Geoffrey
Moore**; the "own the empty corner" instinct behind competitive positioning echoes **Kim and
Mauborgne's Blue Ocean** thinking; the build-measure-learn spirit of Theme 5 comes from **Steve Blank
and Eric Ries**, which Aulet modifies into the MVBP. Disciplined Entrepreneurship is the synthesis — a
single ordered method that pulls these threads into 24 concrete steps.

**And you have already met most of it.** Chapters 1 through 4 of this handbook *are* this framework,
applied to how a Foundry venture is actually built. Chapter 1 walked Theme 1 and the front of Theme 2 —
segmentation, the beachhead, End User Profile and Persona, bottom-up TAM, the life-cycle map, the
drawing, the value number, and the next ten customers. Chapters 2 through 4 carried the rest into
practice: build the smallest thing they'll pay for, sell it the way people want to be sold to, and grow
it without breaking it. This chapter is where the full detail lives — the reference you come back to when
a lane names a step and you want to know precisely what it's doing and why.

The test of the whole thing: at any moment you should be able to say which of the six questions you've
genuinely answered with evidence, and which you're still assuming. Assumptions aren't failures —
pretending they're answers is.
