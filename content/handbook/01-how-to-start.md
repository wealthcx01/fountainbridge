# Chapter 1 — How to Start

Most ventures don't die because the product was bad. They die because nobody needed
it — or because the founder built for everyone and so reached no one. This chapter is
about not dying that way.

There is one rule underneath everything here. Bill Aulet, who runs the entrepreneurship
centre at MIT and wrote the framework we lean on in this handbook, puts it as plainly as
it can be put: **the single necessary and sufficient condition for a business is a paying
customer.** Not a product, not a technology, not a clever plan, not a great team — a
person who hands over money. Everything in this chapter is a disciplined way of finding
that person before you spend six months building for a person who isn't there.

We'll use one example the whole way through so the ideas stay concrete: imagine you want
to help **independent dental practices stop losing money to missed appointments**. Keep
that in mind — we'll narrow it, sharpen it, and pressure-test it as we go.

A note on how this works. The steps below look linear. They aren't. Aulet calls the real
motion **"spiralling"** — you'll reach step nine, learn something, and come back to
sharpen step two. That's not failure; that's the method working. The studio keeps every
version, so you can always see how your thinking moved.

At literal zero you don't need a perfect plan, a team, funding, or a polished product. You
need a problem worth exploring, a customer you can learn from, and enough momentum to take
the next step. That's the whole job of this chapter.

---

## Is it the right time?

Almost everyone should try to start a company at some point. It's hard, it takes a while
to pay off, and it sometimes carries real risk — but at the end you have something you
built yourself. The honest question is rarely *should I ever start*; it's *is now the right
time for me to make an attempt?*

You don't need permission to start, but you should be honest about the signals. Founders
who start from a position of strength usually have some mix of these:

- **You've lived the problem.** You've felt the pain yourself, or watched people close to
  you feel it. This is the single best starting point — it's why our dental example works
  better if you've actually run a front desk and watched the calendar bleed.
- **You understand a customer or need better than most people do.** Not just that the
  problem exists, but *why* it's hard, which parts are merely annoying and which parts are
  genuinely expensive.
- **You have an unfair advantage.** You know something most people don't, you can reach a
  group most people can't, or you can build something most people can't. Aulet's word for
  the durable version of this is your **Core** — hold that thought, we'll come back to it.
- **Something just changed.** A new technology, a new regulation, a new behaviour has made
  something possible (or necessary) that wasn't a year ago. Change opens doors that were
  shut.
- **You keep coming back to it.** You've tried to ignore the problem and you can't. That
  quiet pull — returning to the same idea after talking yourself out of it — is one of the
  more reliable signals that you've found something worth chasing.
- **You can give it real focus.** Not necessarily full-time on day one, but enough that
  the venture gets your best hours, not your leftover ones. If you have some slack for a
  side project, or you're at a point where you're willing to take on risk, that's the
  window.

If several of these are true, it's probably the right time. If none of them are yet,
that's useful to know now rather than in month eight.

## Where good ideas come from

Ideas aren't lightning. Most good ones start with a **problem** — because a problem gives
you something real to test. If people already feel pain, already spend money, already use
awkward workarounds, or already complain about the current options, you have signal. You
may still be wrong about the *solution*, but you're at least pointed at something that
matters. Startup ideas come from three ordinary places.

### 1. Problems you understand

The friction you or the people around you hit every day. Under-rated because it feels too
obvious — but "obvious to you" is exactly the edge. Three places to look:

- **A problem from your previous work.** The strongest source. You were inside a company
  or industry, watched people struggle with the same workflow over and over, and realised
  the tools weren't good enough. These ideas come with *context* — you know the customer,
  you know the language they use, you know what they've already tried, and you know which
  parts of the problem are merely annoying and which parts are genuinely expensive. If
  you've run a dental front desk, you already know a no-show isn't just irritating; it's a
  £150 hole in the day that the owner will ask about.
- **Something from your personal life.** You notice an annoyance and wonder why nobody has
  fixed it. This works especially well when the pain is frequent, obvious, and shared by
  many people. Uber is the classic case: getting a taxi used to be unreliable, opaque, and
  frustrating. The problem wasn't hidden — it was sitting on the street.
- **Customer research.** You pick a market, interview dozens of people, and look for
  repeated problems. This can work, but it's harder than it sounds. People aren't always
  good at describing their own problems — they describe symptoms, not causes, and ask for
  features, not solutions. Treat interviews as a way to *sharpen* a problem you already
  half-understand, not as a substitute for your own judgment.

### 2. Something newly possible

The iPhone created the mobile-app boom. Cheap cloud infrastructure let people start
software companies without buying servers. Modern AI is opening new ways to build, search,
automate, and generate. When a capability arrives, things that were too expensive, too
slow, or too awkward suddenly become practical — and that creates openings.

But the technology is the *unlock*, not the reason a customer cares. **"We're using AI" is
not a startup idea.** "We help paralegals review discovery documents in half the time"
might be. Always map the new capability to a real customer pain. The useful question:
*what became newly possible that customers already wanted?*

### 3. A specific opening

A gap a particular group has that nobody serves well — the customers everyone else finds
too small, too awkward, or too unglamorous to bother with. Sometimes it comes from access
you have that others don't: your family, your job, your location, your relationships, your
reputation; a distribution channel others can't easily copy; a regulatory change you
understand before the market reacts; a niche industry you've lived inside.

This kind of edge is worth naming. We call it **alpha** — opportunity you can reach that's
higher than what the market at large can reach. Alpha gives you a head start, but it isn't
a company on its own. **Use alpha as a wedge to build something durable.** A relationship
can win you the first customer, but you still need repeatable value. A regulatory change
can create urgency, but you still need a product. A unique channel can get attention, but
the product still has to retain the customers it brings you.

Notice what's *not* on the list of idea sources: "a huge market." A big market is where
competition lives. You're looking for a specific, under-served pocket of it — which is the
whole of the section after next.

## Pressure-test the idea

Not every idea is worth pursuing. Before you fall in love, hold it up to four lights.

- **Is there real pain?** Pain means the customer *already cares* — they're losing money,
  wasting time, missing opportunities, taking on risk, or bound to a workflow they hate.
  A "nice-to-have" can work, but it's much harder to sell; a painful problem pulls the
  product into the market. The test: *what does the customer do today because your product
  doesn't exist — and would they pay you to stop?* If they're using spreadsheets, hiring
  people, duct-taping tools together, paying consultants, or tolerating a bad incumbent,
  that's a strong signal. A dental practice losing £150 every time a chair sits empty is
  real pain; "it'd be nice to have a prettier calendar" is not.
- **Is there a market?** Enough customers who feel that pain to build a business on. The
  market should be **big already, or small but likely to become huge.** A big existing
  market means demand is proven — the opening is to serve one segment better than the
  current options. A small, fast-growing market can be even better: new technologies,
  rules, and behaviours often create markets before they look large on a spreadsheet, and
  you can enter before the answer is obvious. Don't pick a market *just* because it's
  large — pick one where you can name a reachable customer with a painful problem and a
  budget. Too small, and you'll struggle to grow revenue no matter how good the product is.
  We'll size this properly below.
- **Is there founder–market fit?** Are *you* the right person to build this — by
  experience, access, or insight? Maybe you worked in the industry, maybe you *are* the
  customer, maybe you have relationships that get you early meetings, maybe you understand
  the buyer's culture and constraints better than an outsider. The practical version of
  the question: *do you have a realistic shot at actually starting this company?* With
  software in 2026, the answer is usually yes. But a non-lawyer trying to start a law firm
  probably doesn't — and customers can smell a founder who doesn't know their world.
- **Do you have a unique insight?** Something you believe about this market that most
  people don't yet — and that turns out to be true. It might be that a segment is
  underserved because incumbents chase bigger customers upmarket; that a workflow can now
  be automated because of a new model; that buyers dislike the current tools far more than
  outsiders realise; or that something that worked in one country or industry will work in
  another. Without an insight, you're probably entering the market the same way everyone
  else sees it — not impossible, but a harder road. This is the seed of your Core, and
  eventually of your **moat** (Chapter 7). Write it in one sentence. If you can't, you may
  not have one yet.

**Positive signals** — the idea gets stronger if any of these are true:

- You're building something *you personally want*.
- It only recently became possible.
- There are successful analogs elsewhere (another market, another country, an adjacent
  industry).
- Customers have already hacked together their own solution — an ugly spreadsheet, a
  whiteboard, a part-time person. A workaround is proof the pain is worth paying to remove.
- The market seems to *pull the product out of you* — people describe the problem before
  you do, and ask when they can have it.

**Common mistakes** — the traps that make founders walk past good ideas:

- **Avoiding ideas that seem too hard.** Hard ideas can be *good* ideas: fewer people
  attempt them, and it's often easier to rally talent and capital around something
  ambitious than around something small.
- **Avoiding boring industries.** Many of the best opportunities aren't glamorous. They
  live in logistics, insurance, construction, compliance, accounting, healthcare
  administration, manufacturing, and legal workflows — places where the software is old
  and the customers have real budgets. Boring problems make excellent businesses. Dental
  admin is not glamorous; that's part of why it's a good place to look.
- **Being too afraid of competitors.** Competition usually means the problem is real. The
  question is not "are there competitors?" but "why will customers choose *us*, now?"
- **Waiting for the perfect idea.** You'll learn more from a month of serious customer
  conversations and rough prototyping than from a year of abstract brainstorming.

The clearest bad signal cuts the other way: everyone says "nice idea" and nobody changes
anything they do.

## Narrow to a beachhead

This is the most important move in the chapter, and the one first-time founders resist
hardest. **You must pick one small market and go all-in on it.** Not "dental practices and
also physios and maybe vets." One.

Aulet borrows a term from Geoffrey Moore for this: the **beachhead market**. Think of the
D-Day landings — you don't invade the whole coastline at once, you take one beach, win it
completely, and use that foothold to push inland. Your beachhead is the one market you can
dominate first, so that dominating it gives you the strength (the references, the cash, the
learning) to take the next market over.

*(You'll hear the same idea called a **wedge** elsewhere — the idea you use to split open
a market. Beachhead and wedge are the same thing: the first, narrow customer group you can
find, understand, sell, and use as proof for the larger opportunity. We'll use "beachhead"
throughout, but treat "wedge" as a synonym when you meet it.)*

The startup idea is the wedge; the market is the world around it. Which market you pick is
not a detail — it decides **who you learn from, how you sell, how much customers can pay,
how large the company can eventually become, and what kind of product you have to build.**
A good early market usually has most of these traits:

- The customer is **easy to describe**.
- The pain is **frequent or expensive**.
- The buyer **has budget**.
- You can **reach customers without a giant sales team**.
- The current alternatives are **bad, expensive, slow, or outdated**.
- The market is **changing in a way that creates an opening**.

### Start narrow — narrower than feels comfortable

The instinct is to keep the market big, because you want the company to be big. But the
best way to *become* big is usually to start with a small group that cares intensely, win
it completely, and expand outward. Specificity is what lets you build, sell, and learn:
when you know exactly who the customer is, you know where to find them, what language to
use, which features matter, and — just as usefully — who to ignore.

So don't say your customer is "small businesses." Say it's "independent dental practices
of two-to-four chairs that lose real money to missed appointments." (In a different
venture, you wouldn't say "creators" — you'd say "YouTube educators selling paid cohorts
who need to convert viewers into students.") The picture should get sharp enough to see a
face.

Here's the same narrowing drawn out — small enough to *reach*, inside a market big enough
to *matter*:

```
BROAD MARKET
  independent businesses that lose money to no-shows
  → everyone who could theoretically buy from you; too broad to sell into directly
        │
        ▼
REACHABLE SEGMENT
  independent dental practices
  → a segment you can actually reach, with budget and real pain
        │
        ▼
FIRST WEDGE (the beachhead)
  two-to-four-chair private practices losing money to missed appointments
  → the one group you can find, sell, and win first — and use as proof
```

### How to get there

1. **Brainstorm widely, then narrow.** List every distinct group who might have this
   problem — aim for a dozen. Crucially, segment by the **actual human who uses the
   thing**, not the organisation. "Dental practices" isn't a segment; "the front-desk
   coordinator at a two-to-four-chair private practice" is. A school doesn't use a
   textbook — teachers do.
2. **Score each segment honestly** against questions like: Can these customers pay? Can you
   actually reach them? Do they have an urgent reason to buy? Can you deliver them a whole,
   working solution soon? Is there entrenched competition that would crush you? If you win
   them, does it help you win the next group?
3. **Check it's really one market.** Aulet's test: the customers buy in similar ways, they
   get value in similar ways, and — most importantly — **they talk to each other.** Word of
   mouth between customers is what makes a beachhead compound. Dental-practice owners go to
   the same conferences and swap tools; that's a market. "Small businesses" is not.

Then pick one and **deselect the rest.** This feels like closing doors. It is. Aulet's
line, and ours: *"I have yet to see a founder focus too much — it is always the other way
around."* And remember the wedge is not the final market — it's the first customer group
you can win, and the proof you'll point at when you go after the larger opportunity.

> Customer Profile — before you write a single line of code, and keeps it as a working
> document every agent lane can read. When a lane drafts a landing page or a first
> outreach, it writes to *this* customer, not a generic one, because your beachhead is
> written down where the machine can see it.

## Get specific about who

Now go narrower still — inside the beachhead, to the person. Aulet splits this into two
deliberate steps, and the difference between them matters:

**The End User Profile** is a rich *composite* of your target user. Go well past age and
job title. Capture what they're trying to achieve, **what they're most afraid of**, who
their hero is, what they read and watch, where they gather (Aulet calls these their
"watering holes"), and what a normal day looks like. For our example: a coordinator, 30s,
runs the front desk single-handed, dreads the owner asking why revenue dipped, lives in the
practice-management software all day, reads a couple of dental-admin Facebook groups.

**The Persona** is different: it's **one real, named person** who best fits that profile —
not an average, an actual human you can point to. Aulet is strict about this: there is only
one Persona, and you build the whole team's understanding around them. Pin them to the
wall. The most important thing you write down is their **priorities, in order** — the thing
that keeps them awake, the thing that gets them praised or fired. And treat what they *say*
with care: watch what they *do*. People will tell you they care about the environment and
then buy on price every time.

Getting End User Profile and Persona confused is the most common way to misread this
framework — the profile is the type, the Persona is the person.

## Size it honestly

You need to know the market is big enough to matter and small enough to win. The number is
the **Total Addressable Market (TAM)**: the annual revenue you'd earn if you had *100% of
your beachhead* — dollars per year, not a count of customers.

Do it **bottom-up first** — Aulet calls this "counting noses." Start from real, countable
users: how many independent practices of your target size exist in your first region?
Multiply by what each would realistically pay per year. A top-down number from an industry
report ("the global dental software market is $X billion") is worth a sanity-check at most —
it always flatters you, because *"I have never seen a real live customer hiding in a cell on
a spreadsheet."*

As a rough guide, a beachhead TAM around **£15–75m a year** is a healthy target: big enough
to build a real company, small enough that you can plausibly own it. Far smaller and it may
not sustain a venture; far larger and you've probably not narrowed enough, or you've
wandered back into the whole coastline.

## Walk the whole journey

Before you design anything, map the customer's **entire** experience with your product —
what Aulet calls the **Full Life Cycle Use Case**. Not just "they use the app," but all of
it: how they first realise they have the problem, how they hear about you, how they weigh
you up, how they buy, how they get set up, how they use it, how they decide it was worth it,
how they pay, how they get help, and how they tell someone else.

Do this in the customer's shoes, not yours — founders systematically overestimate how
excited people are and how easily they'll switch. And map their *current* way of doing
things first, so you can slot into their day rather than demand they rip it up. Our
coordinator already chases no-shows somehow today (probably a manual phone call and a
sticky note). If your product doesn't obviously beat the sticky note in week one, it loses.

---

## Sketch the product — and its value

Only now do you describe the product, and even here you **don't build it yet.** Aulet's
point: a high-level spec is *"at its core, a drawing."* A storyboard, a wireframe, a rough
picture of the screens. The drawing forces you and anyone helping you to agree on what the
thing actually is, without the cost and the emotional attachment that come the moment you
start building.

Then turn it into a one-page **value proposition** the Persona would recognise — and make
it a **number tied to their top priority.** Not "our software is powerful." Instead: the
"as-is" world versus the "possible" world, in their units. For the coordinator whose
priority is revenue the owner can see: *"Practices lose an average of £2,000 a month to
no-shows. In our pilots, we cut that by 40%."* And **under-promise** — quote a number you're
confident you can beat. Over-claiming is the fastest way to lose a serious buyer. Talk
about the outcome, not the feature set: nobody buys a reminder system because it has
filters; they buy it because it stops the chair sitting empty.

---

## Find your next ten

Here's the test that turns a nice theory into evidence. Beyond your one Persona, find **ten
more** real potential customers who fit the profile, and go talk to them. This is the first
real exam for everything above.

- Stay in **inquiry mode**, not sales mode. You're there to learn, not to convince.
- Show them the journey map, the drawing, and the value number. Watch their faces.
- **Welcome the negative.** A customer who tells you exactly why it won't work for them is
  a gift — they care enough to explain. "Yeah, looks great" from everyone usually means
  nobody cares.
- Look for **patterns**, not single reactions. One person hating it means little; five
  people tripping over the same thing means everything.
- If they're genuinely keen, ask softly for a non-binding commitment — *"if this existed,
  would you commit to buying it?"* A hesitant "maybe" is really a no — push gently for a
  real answer. Someone offering to pre-pay is gold.
- And ask the compounding question: **who else has this problem?** A warm "yes, and my
  friend across town has it worse" is the start of your lead list — and a second check that
  your customers really do talk to each other.

And the honest part: **if you can't find ten people who light up, reconsider the
beachhead.** You cannot will a market into existing. This is also where you often discover a
*better* Persona than the one you started with — that's the spiralling working, not a
setback.

## Pick a name and a domain

Naming feels important because it's *visible* — and it is. You're building a brand, and
people will come to know the company by it. A good name is **simple, memorable, easy to
spell, and flexible enough to survive changes in the product.** You don't need a perfect
name. You need one that doesn't get in the way. A few rules:

- Avoid names people can't spell after hearing them once.
- Avoid names that lock you into a tiny feature — you'll outgrow them.
- Avoid names that sound too similar to an existing company.
- Avoid cleverness that only lands after you explain it.
- Check basic trademark risk before you get attached.
- Make sure you can get a reasonable domain *and* the social handles.

On the domain: a **`.com` is ideal but not required at the very beginning.** A clean
alternative is fine if it lets you move. Good options are `.co`, `.app`, `.io`, `.ai`,
`.dev`, `.tv` (if you're in media), or `.xyz` (if you're in crypto). Steer clear of the
more obscure TLDs — they rank lower in search and quietly confuse customers. A common trick
when the bare name is taken is to prefix a familiar word: `try-` or `use-` in front of the
name. Whatever you do, **don't spend a month here.** Hold the name lightly, pick something
workable, and change it later if you must — you can't negotiate for the perfect domain
before you've talked to a single customer.

## Take the leap

The beginning of a company is about turning uncertainty into motion. Don't try to solve the
next five years — solve the next week. Start.

Not "raise a round and hire a team" — start by proving you can deliver value to your next
customer next week. The rest of this handbook is how you do that: build the smallest thing
they'll actually pay for (Chapter 2), sell it the way people actually want to be sold to
(Chapter 3), and grow it without breaking it (Chapter 4).

---

## What you should have at the end of Chapter 1

- A clear-eyed answer to **why now** — the signals that make this the right time for you to
  attempt this venture.
- One **beachhead market** (a.k.a. your wedge), chosen and written down — with the dozen
  you rejected, and the traits that make it reachable.
- An **End User Profile** and a single, named **Persona** with their priorities in order.
- A **bottom-up TAM** for the beachhead, and the honest verdict on whether it's big enough
  to matter and small enough to win.
- A **full-journey map**, a **rough drawing** of the product, and a one-line **value
  proposition** stated as a number tied to the Persona's top priority.
- **Ten real customers** contacted — and a clear read on whether they light up or not.
- A one-sentence **unique insight** you're betting on. Keep it close: it's the seed of the
  Core and the moat you'll build in Chapter 7.
- A **name and a workable domain** — chosen quickly, held lightly.

If you have those, you haven't guaranteed success — but you've earned the right to build,
which is more than most ventures ever do.
