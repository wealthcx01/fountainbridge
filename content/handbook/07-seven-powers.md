# Chapter 7 — The 7 Powers

You can do everything in the first six chapters right — find a real customer, build the
smallest thing they'll pay for, sell it honestly, grow it without breaking — and still
lose. Not because the product got worse, but because it got *noticed*. The moment you're
visibly making money, other people want that money too. They copy the feature, undercut the
price, hire away the idea. If nothing stops them, the profit you worked for leaks away to the
whole market, and you end up on a treadmill: running hard every quarter, defending nothing.

This chapter is about getting off the treadmill. The framework comes from **Hamilton
Helmer**, an investor and strategist whose book *7 Powers* is the clearest thing written on
one question: *what makes a business durably valuable?* Not valuable this quarter — durable,
year after year. Helmer's answer is that there are exactly seven structural advantages that
do this, and no more. He calls them **Powers**, and the whole of strategy, in his framing, is
the study of how you get one. What we add is the Bruntsfield *application* — how a Foundry
venture builds these Powers across the journey you walked in Chapters 1–4 — and we flag it as
ours.

---

## What "Power" actually means

Helmer defines Power precisely: it is **the set of conditions needed for persistent
differential returns.** Unpack that in plain words.

- **Returns** means the money the business earns for its owner over time — its free cash flow.
- **Differential** means *more than it costs you to be in business at all.* Every business has
  a bar to clear: the return investors could get just by putting their money somewhere else of
  similar risk. Clearing that bar — earning genuinely more than your **cost of capital** (the
  price of the money tied up in running the thing) — is a differential return. Merely staying
  alive is not.
- **Persistent** is the hard word. It means the differential *keeps happening* — competitors
  can see you earning it and still can't erase it.

That last word is where most businesses fail, and it's why Helmer says a Power always has
**two halves at once.** You need both, or you have nothing durable:

- A **Benefit** — something that materially improves your cash flow. There are only three ways
  to do that: you can charge **higher prices**, carry **lower costs**, or tie up **less
  capital** (less money locked into stock, kit, and staff before customers pay you). A Benefit
  is any real, measurable improvement on one of those three.
- A **Barrier** — the reason a competitor *can't or won't* copy the Benefit away, even though
  they can see exactly what you're doing.

Here is the whole heart of the framework in one line, and it's worth reading twice: **a
Benefit without a Barrier is competed away.** On its own, a Benefit is just a good quarter.
The moment rivals notice, they pile in — copying, discounting, poaching — until the extra
profit is *arbitraged out.* ("Arbitrage" is the economist's word for exactly that pile-in:
when people spot easy money and rush at it until the easy money is gone.) The Barrier is the
only thing that stops the pile-in. So the Benefit is the easy half. **The Barrier is the
strategy.**

Foundry ventures are small and co-created. We can't out-spend anyone. That makes this
non-optional: we win by building Barriers early and on purpose, while they're still cheap to
build. Which brings us to the single most useful idea in the whole book.

---

## The idea that changes everything: timing

Most people treat "moats" as something you either have or you don't. Helmer's real insight is
subtler and far more useful to a founder: **each Power can only be *established* during a
particular window of a business's life.** Miss the window and the door often shuts for good.

He splits a business's life into three moments: **Origination** (the founding, when the model
itself is still a choice), **Takeoff** (rapid growth, when volume and users compound fast), and
**Stability** (maturity, when the game is defending what you have).

Different Powers belong to different moments. **Counter-Positioning** and the **Cornered
Resource** are *origination* moves — you either choose them at the start or you usually can't
choose them at all. **Scale Economies, Network Economies,** and **Switching Costs** are built
during *takeoff*, as growth lays the barrier down. **Branding** and **Process Power** can only
harden in *stability*, because both need years of consistent work no newcomer can
fast-forward.

For a founder, this turns strategy from a vague ambition ("build a moat someday") into a
concrete question you can answer *today*: **given where my venture is right now, which Power is
still open to me — and am I building it?** That's the question the rest of this chapter is
built to answer.

## The seven, one at a time

We take them in Helmer's own numbering. For each: the Benefit, the Barrier, a crisp example
(most are Helmer's own), and — the Bruntsfield angle — *when* in a venture's life you can
actually build it.

### 1. Scale Economies

**Benefit.** Your cost per unit falls as you get bigger. Some large chunk of your costs is
fixed — you pay it once whether you serve a hundred customers or a million — so the more you
sell, the thinner that cost spreads across each sale. You can then profitably price where a
smaller rival simply can't follow.

**Barrier.** To match your costs, a challenger would have to match your scale — and buying that
much market share, fast, against an entrenched leader, costs far more than it could ever earn
back. So they rationally don't try. Helmer's word for this is the prohibitive *share-gain
cost.*

**Example.** Netflix spends enormously on content, but spreads that one bill across a vast
subscriber base, so its cost *per subscriber* is tiny. A new rival with a fraction of the
subscribers pays a similar bill over far fewer people, and can never make the numbers work.

**When you can build it.** *Takeoff* — a **Chapter 4** power, rarely a young venture's first.
You earn it by winning your beachhead so completely that you become its volume leader, then
designing fixed costs (content, model, infrastructure) to spread over a growing base rather
than recur with every customer.

### 2. Network Economies

**Benefit.** The product gets *more valuable to each user as more users join.* A phone is
useless if you're the only person who owns one; priceless when everyone does. The value comes
from the other users, not from a new feature.

**Barrier.** A latecomer is trapped. To attract users they need to already have users — and
they don't. Meanwhile every user you add makes your product better and the gap *wider*, not
narrower. A challenger would have to move an entire network across at once, which almost never
happens.

**Example.** An early marketplace or messaging network: once buyers and sellers (or friends)
are all in one place, no rival offering the same thing to an empty room can lure them out.

**When you can build it.** *Conditions early, harvest at takeoff.* Make sharing and connection
*native* to the product from the first version — a lone-user product can't grow a network
bolted on later — but the barrier only compounds once the base is large. A **Chapter 4** power
seeded in **Chapter 2**.

### 3. Counter-Positioning

**Benefit.** You adopt a genuinely superior business model — a new way of pricing, delivering,
or serving — that a strong incumbent *cannot* copy without damaging the business they already
have.

**Barrier.** Helmer calls it *collateral damage.* The incumbent can see your model perfectly
well. Copying it would cannibalise their existing profits, so their own board rationally
*refuses* to follow. Their strength becomes their cage. This is the rare Power where the
competitor is *unwilling* rather than unable — and it's often a small venture's single best
weapon against a large one.

**Example.** Vanguard's cheap index funds versus traditional active fund managers. The active
managers understood index funds fine — but to compete, they'd have had to abandon the fat fees
that were their whole business. Their profits were the thing stopping them.

**When you can build it.** *Origination* — a founding choice, made at the very start
(**Chapter 1**), which is why your business-model decisions there were strategic, not just
tactical. Bruntsfield's own co-creation model is counter-positioning: agents doing the work a
traditional agency bills by the hour means an incumbent can't match our price without
cannibalising the billable hours that fund them.

### 4. Switching Costs

**Benefit.** Once a customer is in, leaving is expensive for *them* — their data lives in your
product, their team is trained on it, their other tools plug into it, and moving risks breaking
all of that. So they'll pay more to stay than a rival would have to charge to lure them out.

**Barrier.** To win your customer, a competitor must not only match you but also *compensate the
customer for the entire cost of switching* — retraining, migration, risk. Usually that's
uneconomic, so they don't bother.

**Example.** Enterprise software like SAP: once a company has run its whole operation on it for
years, ripping it out is a multi-year, multi-million-pound project. The incumbent can raise
prices and still keep the customer.

**When you can build it.** *Built into how you design the product* — a **Chapter 2** decision,
with the barrier setting during takeoff. Embed into the customer's real workflow rather than
sitting beside it, and let them invest history, configuration, and results into it so it grows
*more theirs* over time. Earn this through delivered value — never by holding data hostage.

### 5. Branding

**Benefit.** A customer will pay more — or trust you faster, or choose you without shopping
around — for an offering that is *objectively identical* to a rival's, purely because of what
your name means to them.

**Barrier.** Helmer's word is *hysteresis* — a fancy term for "this can only be built slowly,
over years, and can't be rushed." A reputation is the accumulated residue of doing what you
said you'd do, again and again. A competitor with all the money in the world can't buy your
history; they can only start living their own, which takes just as long.

**Example.** Tiffany can sell a diamond of the same objective quality as a discount jeweller for
a large premium, because a century of consistent signalling made the blue box mean something.

**When you can build it.** *Starts at Chapter 3, hardens in stability.* The work begins the day
you first sell — every honest interaction is a deposit, every over-claim a withdrawal — but the
*barrier* is real only after years of consistent delivery. This is why the studio's rule is
**fail loud, surface everything**: brand is the accumulated trust of every honest interaction,
and one bad send spends years of it.

### 6. Cornered Resource

**Benefit.** You have preferential access to something genuinely valuable and scarce that your
rivals simply can't get — a person or team, a dataset, a patent, a licence, an exclusive
supply or relationship — and on terms nobody else can match.

**Barrier.** Helmer's word is *fiat* — an exclusive right or a plain scarcity. The resource is
locked to you: a patent others can't work around, an exclusive agreement, or an asset there's
only one of. They can't copy it because they can't *have* it.

**Example.** Pixar's early run of hits rested on a small, cohesive group of exceptional
creative leaders who chose to build together and couldn't simply be hired away — a cornered
resource in human form.

**When you can build it.** *Origination.* The scarce thing is almost always secured at or near
founding, before competitors even know the market exists (**Chapter 1**). For a Foundry venture
it's often a **proprietary dataset or partnership** — the "Core" you named in Chapter 6 — locked
in early. It's partly structural to Foundry too: the founder is a genuine domain insider
co-building with us, on a shared substrate no single founder could build alone.

### 7. Process Power

**Benefit.** Your organisation simply *does something complex better* than anyone else — lower
cost, better product, or both — because of how it works internally, not because of any single
thing you could point at and copy.

**Barrier.** Hysteresis again, but of the deepest kind. The processes are tacit, tangled, and
improved over so many years that even a competitor who could watch you all day couldn't
reproduce them quickly — it took you a decade of ten-thousand small refinements to get here, and
it would take them one too.

**Example.** The Toyota Production System. Rivals have toured the factories, read the books, and
hired the consultants for decades — and still can't fully match it, because it lives in the
accumulated habits of the whole organisation.

**When you can build it.** *Stability, and only over a long time.* This is the longest game of
all, and it's where Foundry's substrate quietly helps: the lanes, gstack's plan-review-ship
discipline, the approval matrix, and gbrain's accumulating memory *are* a process that
compounds — each new venture inherits a maturing operating machine rather than reinventing one.

## Mapping the powers onto your journey

Here's the Bruntsfield point, and it's what makes this a chapter rather than a summary. The
seven Powers aren't a menu to browse someday — they line up, in order, with the venture journey
you've already walked:

- **Chapter 1 — Origination.** You made your *counter-positioning* choice (the business model an
  incumbent can't copy without hurting itself) and, if you had one, secured your *cornered
  resource* (the scarce dataset, relationship, or right). These doors are widest at the very
  start and tend to close later. This is why the "unique insight" you were told to write down in
  one sentence in Chapter 1 mattered so much: it's the seed of both.
- **Chapter 2 — Build.** You designed *switching costs* into the product — by embedding into the
  customer's real workflow and letting them invest their history and configuration into it — and
  you laid the *conditions for network economies* if your product has them, by making connection
  native rather than bolted on.
- **Chapter 3 — Sell.** You began depositing into your *brand* with every honest, delivered
  promise. The barrier won't be real for years, but the account opens now.
- **Chapter 4 — Scale.** *Scale economies* and *network economies* arrive with growth — you only
  earn them once you're the volume leader in a beachhead you've genuinely won.
- **Stability — later.** Your brand barrier hardens, and *process power* slowly emerges from how
  your venture has learned to operate.

So the question at the end of this chapter isn't "which of the seven do I want?" It's: **which
Power does my *current stage* allow me to build — and is my next move depositing into it or
spending it?** A founder at Chapter 1 who spends their energy dreaming about scale economies is
looking at the wrong door. Their door is counter-positioning, and it's open right now.

> **In the studio:** name your intended Power where the lanes can read it, the way you named your
> beachhead. When a lane drafts product, outreach, or a pricing change, it can then ask the right
> question — *does this deepen the Power we're building, or spend it?* — instead of optimising a
> number in a vacuum.

## Core and Power: close cousins, not the same thing

You've met a near-relative of this idea already. In Chapter 6, Bill Aulet's Disciplined
Entrepreneurship has a step called **Define Your Core** (Step 10): name the *one* thing you'll do
better than anyone, that's hard to copy — your source of enduring advantage. That sounds a lot
like a Power, and the two are deeply related. But keep the distinction sharp, because it's easy
to blur:

- Aulet's **Core** is a claim about *you* — a capability, the thing you're best at, the reason
  you believe you'll keep winning.
- Helmer's **Power** is a claim about the *market structure around you* — specifically, the
  Barrier that stops rivals arbitraging your advantage away.

A Core is the raw material; a Power is what you build from it once you've added a Barrier. Your
Core might *become* a Cornered Resource (if it's a scarce, locked-in asset) or, over years, a
Process Power (if it's a way of operating no one can copy). But a Core with no Barrier — a thing
you do brilliantly that anyone could learn to do too — is just a good idea waiting to be
competed away. The Core tells you *what* you're great at. The Power tells you *why the market
can't take it from you.* You need both; this chapter is about the half Aulet's step points
toward but doesn't finish.

---

## You only need one

A final relief: **you don't need all seven.** You need *one*, deliberately built while its window
is open, defending a business you've already proven with the discipline of Chapters 1–6. Most
enduring companies rest on one or two Powers, not a stack of them.

So pick early — for a young venture it's almost always counter-positioning or switching costs,
because those are the ones a small player can build first — and let it become the lens you hold
over every other decision. Value you can't defend is a treadmill. A Power is how a Foundry
venture steps off it.

---

## What you should have at the end of Chapter 7

- A clear grasp that a durable advantage is always a **Benefit *and* a Barrier** — and that a
  Benefit without a Barrier is competed away.
- The **one Power** you're deliberately building, named and written down where the lanes can see
  it — chosen to fit the stage your venture is actually at.
- An honest read on your **timing**: which Powers are still open to you (origination moves if
  you're early; scale and network if you're growing) and which have closed.
- The link back to your **Core** (Chapter 6) and your one-sentence **unique insight** (Chapter
  1) — and a clear view of the Barrier that turns that Core into something the market can't take
  from you.
- For every major move ahead, one question in your pocket: *does this deposit into my Power, or
  spend it?*

Get that, and you're no longer just building something people want. You're building something
you can *keep* — which is the whole difference between a good quarter and a company.
