# Chapter 7 — The 7 Powers

You can do everything in the first six chapters right — find a real customer, build the
smallest thing they'll pay for, sell it honestly, grow it without breaking — and still
lose. Not because the product got worse, but because it got *noticed*. The moment you're
visibly making money, other people want that money too. They copy the feature, undercut the
price, hire away the idea. If nothing stops them, the profit you worked for leaks away to the
whole market, and you end up on a treadmill: running hard every quarter, defending nothing.

This chapter is about getting off the treadmill. The framework comes from **Hamilton
Helmer**, an investor and strategist whose book *7 Powers* is the clearest thing written on
one question: *what makes a business durably valuable?* Not valuable this quarter — durable,
year after year. Helmer's answer is that there are exactly seven structural advantages that
do this, and no more. He calls them **Powers**, and the whole of strategy, in his framing, is
the study of how you get one. What we add is the Bruntsfield *application* — how a Foundry
venture builds these Powers across the journey you walked in Chapters 1–4 — and we flag it as
ours.

---

## What "Power" actually means

Helmer defines Power precisely: it is **the set of conditions needed for persistent
differential returns.** Unpack that in plain words.

- **Returns** means the money the business earns for its owner over time — its free cash flow.
- **Differential** means *more than it costs you to be in business at all.* Every business has a
  bar to clear: what investors could earn by putting the same money somewhere else of similar
  risk. Earning genuinely more than that **cost of capital** (the price of the money tied up in
  running the thing) is a differential return. Merely staying alive is not.
- **Persistent** is the hard word: the differential *keeps happening* — competitors can see you
  earning it and still can't erase it.

That last word is where most businesses fail, and it's why Helmer says a Power always has
**two halves at once.** You need both, or you have nothing durable:

- A **Benefit** — something that materially improves your cash flow. There are only three ways
  to do that: you can charge **higher prices**, carry **lower costs**, or tie up **less
  capital** (less money locked into stock, kit, and staff before customers pay you). A Benefit
  is any real, measurable improvement on one of those three.
- A **Barrier** — the reason a competitor *can't or won't* copy the Benefit away, even though
  they can see exactly what you're doing.

Here is the whole heart of the framework in one line, worth reading twice: **a Benefit without
a Barrier is competed away.** Put more bluntly: **a Benefit without a Barrier is a nice
quarter, then a price war.** On its own, a Benefit is just a good quarter. The moment rivals
notice, they pile in — copying, discounting, poaching — until the extra profit is *arbitraged
out.* ("Arbitrage" is the economist's word for exactly that: people spot easy money and rush at
it until it's gone.) The Barrier is the only thing that stops the pile-in. The Benefit is the
easy half. **The Barrier is the strategy.**

Foundry ventures are small and co-created. We can't out-spend anyone. That makes this
non-optional: we win by building Barriers early and on purpose, while they're still cheap to
build. Which brings us to the single most useful idea in the whole book.

---

## The idea that changes everything: timing

Most people treat "moats" as something you either have or you don't. Helmer's real insight is
subtler and far more useful to a founder: **each Power can only be *established* during a
particular window of a business's life.** Miss the window and the door often shuts for good.
Power, in Helmer's framing, is *dynamic*, not a permanent trait you either possess or lack —
which turns strategy into a question of **timing**.

He splits a business's life into three moments: **Origination** (the founding, when the model
itself is still a choice), **Takeoff** (rapid growth, when volume and users compound fast), and
**Stability** (maturity, when the game is defending what you have).

Different Powers belong to different moments. **Counter-Positioning** and the **Cornered
Resource** are *origination* moves — you either choose them at the start or you usually can't
choose them at all. **Scale Economies, Network Economies,** and **Switching Costs** are built
during *takeoff*, as growth lays the barrier down. **Branding** and **Process Power** can only
harden in *stability*, because both need years of consistent work no newcomer can
fast-forward.

For a founder, this turns strategy from a vague ambition ("build a moat someday") into a
concrete question you can answer *today*: **given where my venture is right now, which Power is
still open to me — and am I building it?** For a Foundry venture that question has a precise
starting point: the Power you're building begins its life as the **Core** you named in Chapter 6
(Disciplined Entrepreneurship, Step 10). Name it, then build it while it's still buildable.
That's the question the rest of this chapter is built to answer.

## The seven, one at a time

We take them in Helmer's own numbering. For each we give the same four things: the **Benefit**
(how it improves cash flow), the **Barrier** (why rivals can't or won't copy it), a crisp
**example** (most are Helmer's own), and — the Bruntsfield angle — *when* in a venture's life
you can actually build it, plus *how a Foundry venture earns it* in practice.

A note on the Barriers before we start, because Helmer groups them and the grouping is worth
holding in your head. There are only a few *kinds* of Barrier across all seven Powers:

- **Collateral damage** — the incumbent won't copy you because copying would wound its own
  existing business. (This is Counter-Positioning.)
- **Prohibitive share-gain cost** — a rival *could* match you, but only by buying so much market
  share so fast that the maths never pays back. (This is Scale, Network, and Switching Costs.)
- **Hysteresis** — the advantage can only be laid down slowly, over years, and cannot be
  fast-forwarded with money. (This is Branding and Process Power.)
- **Fiat** — an exclusive right or a plain scarcity locks the resource to you; rivals can't copy
  it because they can't *have* it. (This is the Cornered Resource.)

### 1. Scale Economies

**Benefit.** Your cost per unit falls as you get bigger. Some large chunk of your costs is
fixed — you pay it once whether you serve a hundred customers or a million — so the more you
sell, the thinner that cost spreads across each sale. You can then profitably price where a
smaller rival simply can't follow.

**Barrier.** To match your costs, a challenger would have to match your scale — and buying that
much market share, fast, against an entrenched leader, costs far more than it could ever earn
back. So they rationally don't try. Helmer's word for this is the prohibitive *share-gain
cost.*

**Example.** Netflix spends enormously on content, but spreads that one bill across a vast
subscriber base, so its cost *per subscriber* is tiny. A new rival with a fraction of the
subscribers pays a similar bill over far fewer people, and can never make the numbers work.

**When you can build it.** *Takeoff* — a **Chapter 4** power, and rarely a young venture's
first. Don't kid yourself here early: scale economies arrive only once volume compounds, so a
seed-stage founder claiming them is usually claiming a moat they don't have.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Concentrate on the beachhead until you're genuinely the
volume leader *in it* — not diffuse across many segments, but dominant in one. Then design your
fixed costs so they *spread* across a growing base rather than *recur* with every customer:
content produced once and reused, a model trained once and served many times, infrastructure
that carries more load at little extra cost. The test is simple — as you add customers, does the
big cost get thinner per head, or does it grow in step? Only the first is a scale economy.

### 2. Network Economies

**Benefit.** The product gets *more valuable to each user as more users join.* A phone is
useless if you're the only person who owns one; priceless when everyone does. The value comes
from the other users, not from a new feature.

**Barrier.** A latecomer is trapped. To attract users they need to already have users — and
they don't. Meanwhile every user you add makes your product better and the gap *wider*, not
narrower. A challenger would have to move an entire network across at once, which almost never
happens — the brutal version of the prohibitive *share-gain cost.*

**Example.** An early marketplace or messaging network: once buyers and sellers (or friends)
are all in one place, no rival offering the same thing to an empty room can lure them out.

**When you can build it.** *Conditions early, harvest at takeoff.* Make sharing and connection
*native* to the product from the first version — a lone-user product can't grow a network
bolted on later — but the barrier only compounds once the base is large. A **Chapter 4** power
whose seeds go in during **Chapter 2**.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Build in a genuine reason users benefit from *each other* —
shared data that gets richer with every contributor, matching that improves as both sides fill
in, a community that's more useful the more people are in it. Here the consent-first go-to-market
is a quiet advantage rather than a constraint: because you reach people who've already raised a
hand, each satisfied cohort recruits the next honestly, so the network grows on real referral
rather than bought attention. A network you fed with spam is a network that leaves.

### 3. Counter-Positioning

**Benefit.** You adopt a genuinely superior business model — a new way of pricing, delivering,
or serving — that a strong incumbent *cannot* copy without damaging the business they already
have.

**Barrier.** Helmer calls it *collateral damage.* The incumbent can see your model perfectly
well. Copying it would cannibalise their existing profits, so their own board rationally
*refuses* to follow. Their strength becomes their cage. This is the rare Power where the
competitor is *unwilling* rather than unable — and it's often a small venture's single best
weapon against a large one.

**Example.** Vanguard's cheap index funds versus traditional active fund managers. The active
managers understood index funds fine — but to compete, they'd have had to abandon the fat fees
that were their whole business. Their profits were the thing stopping them.

**When you can build it.** *Origination* — a founding choice, made at the very start
(**Chapter 1**), which is why your business-model decisions there were strategic, not just
tactical. This is the newcomer's move: you can only counter-position *before* you have a legacy
of your own to protect.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Design the model so that success *hurts the incumbent to
imitate* — a pricing, distribution, or consent-first motion their legacy economics simply won't
let them match. Bruntsfield's own co-creation model is exactly this: agents doing the
operational work a traditional agency bills by the hour means an incumbent can't match our price
without cannibalising the billable hours that fund them. The consent-first go-to-market is
counter-positioning too — a firm that lives on cold-outreach volume can't credibly switch to
"we only email people who asked" without disowning its own machine.

### 4. Switching Costs

**Benefit.** Once a customer is in, leaving is expensive for *them* — their data lives in your
product, their team is trained on it, their other tools plug into it, and moving risks breaking
all of that. So they'll pay more to stay than a rival would have to charge to lure them out.

**Barrier.** To win your customer, a competitor must not only match you but also *compensate the
customer for the entire cost of switching* — retraining, migration, risk — on top of matching
your product. Usually that's uneconomic, so they don't bother. Another form of the prohibitive
*share-gain cost*, paid one customer at a time.

**Example.** Enterprise software like SAP: once a company has run its whole operation on it for
years, ripping it out is a multi-year, multi-million-pound project. The incumbent can raise
prices and still keep the customer.

**When you can build it.** *Built into how you design the product* — a **Chapter 2** decision,
with the barrier *setting* during takeoff as customers embed their data, habits, and
integrations into you.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Embed into the customer's real workflow rather than sitting
beside it, and let them *invest* in the product over time — their history, their configuration,
their accumulated results — so it grows steadily *more theirs* the longer they use it. The line
that matters: earn this through delivered value, **never by hostage-taking.** A switching cost
built on "we'll make it painful to leave" is resentment waiting to churn; one built on "leaving
means walking away from everything you've built here" is loyalty.

### 5. Branding

**Benefit.** A customer will pay more — or trust you faster, or choose you without shopping
around — for an offering that is *objectively identical* to a rival's, purely because of what
your name means to them.

**Barrier.** Helmer's word is *hysteresis* — a fancy term for "this can only be built slowly,
over years, and can't be rushed." A reputation is the accumulated residue of doing what you
said you'd do, again and again. A competitor with all the money in the world can't buy your
history; they can only start living their own, which takes just as long.

**Example.** Tiffany can sell a diamond of the same objective quality as a discount jeweller for
a large premium, because a century of consistent signalling made the blue box mean something.

**When you can build it.** *Starts at Chapter 3, hardens in stability.* The work begins the day
you first sell — every honest interaction is a deposit, every over-claim a withdrawal — but the
*barrier* is real only after years of consistent delivery.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Be relentlessly consistent on the one promise the persona
actually cares about (the value you quantified in Chapter 6), and let every interaction pay into
it. For a consent-first venture, brand *is* the accumulated trust of every honest, approved
send — which is exactly why the studio's rule is **fail loud, surface everything.** One bad send
spends years of trust in an afternoon. Branding is the slowest Power to build and the fastest to
break, and that asymmetry is the whole reason we gate every outbound action.

### 6. Cornered Resource

**Benefit.** You have preferential access to something genuinely valuable and scarce that your
rivals simply can't get — a person or team, a dataset, a patent, a licence, an exclusive
supply or relationship — and on terms nobody else can match.

**Barrier.** Helmer's word is *fiat* — an exclusive right or a plain scarcity. The resource is
locked to you: a patent others can't work around, an exclusive agreement, or an asset there's
only one of. They can't copy it because they can't *have* it.

**Example.** Pixar's early run of hits rested on a small, cohesive group of exceptional
creative leaders who chose to build together and couldn't simply be hired away — a cornered
resource in human form.

**When you can build it.** *Origination.* The scarce thing is almost always secured at or near
founding, before competitors even know the market exists (**Chapter 1**).

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** Lock in the scarce thing early — for a Foundry venture it's
most often a **proprietary dataset or partnership**: the "Core" you named in Chapter 6, secured
before rivals know the market exists (a clinic-outcomes dataset that gets better the more
patients use it; an exclusive channel relationship). It's partly structural to Foundry too: the
founder is a genuine domain insider co-building with us, on a shared substrate — gbrain memory,
the contracts, the studio — that no single founder could stand up alone. Be honest, though:
your *brand* and your *team* are not a Cornered Resource on day one, before either is actually
rare. Claiming them early is the classic self-flattery this Power invites.

### 7. Process Power

**Benefit.** Your organisation simply *does something complex better* than anyone else — lower
cost, better product, or both — because of how it works internally, not because of any single
thing you could point at and copy.

**Barrier.** Hysteresis again, of the deepest kind. The processes are tacit, tangled, and
improved over so many years that a competitor who could watch you all day still couldn't
reproduce them quickly — it took you a decade of ten thousand small refinements, and it would
take them one too.

**Example.** The Toyota Production System. Rivals have toured the factories and hired the
consultants for decades and still can't fully match it, because it lives in the accumulated
habits of the whole organisation.

**When you can build it.** *Stability, and only over a long time* — the longest game of all.

**How a Foundry venture earns it.** This is where Foundry's substrate quietly helps, and where
the studio itself is process power in the making. The lanes, gstack's plan → review → ship
discipline, one-ticket-one-branch-one-PR, the adversarial review, the approval matrix, and
gbrain's accumulating memory *are* a compounding process — a way of shipping that a competitor
would have to rebuild their whole culture to match. The payoff is that each new venture inherits
a *maturing* operating machine rather than reinventing one from scratch, so the process barrier
that would take a lone startup a decade to lay down is partly there on day one and deepens with
every venture that runs on it.

## Mapping the powers onto your journey

Here's the Bruntsfield point, and it's what makes this a chapter rather than a summary. The
seven Powers aren't a menu to browse someday — they line up, in order, with the journey you've
already walked:

- **Chapter 1 — Origination.** Your *counter-positioning* choice (a model an incumbent can't copy
  without hurting itself) and, if you had one, your *cornered resource* (a scarce dataset,
  relationship, or right). These doors are widest at the start and close later — which is why the
  one-sentence "unique insight" from Chapter 1 mattered so much: it's the seed of both.
- **Chapter 2 — Build.** *Switching costs*, designed into the product by embedding into the
  customer's workflow, and the *conditions for network economies* if your product has them.
- **Chapter 3 — Sell.** The first deposits into your *brand*, one honest delivered promise at a
  time. The barrier won't be real for years, but the account opens now.
- **Chapter 4 — Scale.** *Scale* and *network economies* arrive with growth — earned only once
  you're the volume leader in a beachhead you've genuinely won.
- **Stability — later.** Your brand barrier hardens, and *process power* emerges from how your
  venture has learned to operate.

So the question at the end of this chapter isn't "which of the seven do I want?" It's: **which
Power does my *current stage* allow me to build — and is my next move depositing into it or
spending it?** A founder at Chapter 1 dreaming about scale economies is looking at the wrong
door. Their door is counter-positioning, and it's open right now.

> **In the studio:** name your intended Power where the lanes can read it, the way you named your
> beachhead. When a lane drafts product, outreach, or a pricing change, it can then ask the right
> question — *does this deepen the Power we're building, or spend it?* — instead of optimising a
> number in a vacuum. A venture with no answer to that question is running on the treadmill:
> growing revenue and defending nothing.

## Core and Power: close cousins, not the same thing

You've met a near-relative of this idea already. In Chapter 6, Bill Aulet's Disciplined
Entrepreneurship has a step called **Define Your Core** (Step 10): name the *one* thing you'll do
better than anyone, hard to copy — your source of enduring advantage. That sounds like a Power,
and the two are deeply related — the Core is the *seed* of a Power. But keep the distinction
sharp:

- Aulet's **Core** is a claim about *you* — a capability, the thing you're best at.
- Helmer's **Power** is a claim about the *market structure around you* — the Barrier that stops
  rivals arbitraging your advantage away.

A Core is the raw material; a Power is what you build from it once you've added a Barrier. Your
Core might *become* a Cornered Resource (if it's a scarce, locked-in asset) or, over years, a
Process Power (a way of operating no one can copy). But a Core with no Barrier — something you do
brilliantly that anyone could learn to do too — is just a good idea waiting to be competed away.
The Core tells you *what* you're great at; the Power tells you *why the market can't take it from
you.* This chapter is about the half Aulet's step points toward but doesn't finish — and it's why
naming the Core early is also naming, in embryo, the Power you intend to defend.

---

## You only need one

A final relief: **you don't need all seven.** You need *one*, deliberately built while its window
is open, defending a business you've already proven with the discipline of Chapters 1–6. Most
enduring companies rest on one or two Powers, not a stack. So pick early — for a young venture
it's almost always counter-positioning or switching costs, the ones a small player can build
first — and let it become the lens you hold over every other decision. Value you can't defend is
a treadmill. A Power is how a Foundry venture steps off it. And remember the order the whole
playbook runs in: Disciplined Entrepreneurship gets you to a proven, profitable beachhead; the
7 Powers are how you keep it from eroding once competitors notice. Build the business with the
one; defend it with the other.

---

## What you should have at the end of Chapter 7

- A clear grasp that a durable advantage is always a **Benefit *and* a Barrier** — a Benefit
  without a Barrier is a nice quarter, then a price war.
- The **one Power** you're deliberately building, written down where the lanes can see it, chosen
  to fit the stage your venture is actually at.
- An honest read on your **timing**: which Powers are still open (origination moves if you're
  early, scale and network if you're growing) and which have closed.
- The link back to your **Core** (Chapter 6) and one-sentence **unique insight** (Chapter 1) —
  and the Barrier that turns that Core into something the market can't take from you.
- One question in your pocket for every move ahead: *does this deposit into my Power, or spend
  it?*

Get that, and you're no longer just building something people want. You're building something
you can *keep* — the whole difference between a good quarter and a company.
